Theorem 3.5.7. Chain Rule for One Independent Variable.
Consider a scalar-valued function \(f: \R^2 \to \R\text{,}\) and a vector-valued function \(\mathbf{r}: \R \to \R^2\) of a single variable. Then the composition \(f \circ \mathbf{r}: \R \to \R\) is a scalar-valued function of a single variable. If \(\mathbf{r}\) is differentiable at \(t_0\text{,}\) and \(f\) is differentiable at \(\mathbf{r}(t_0)\text{,}\) then \(f \circ \mathbf{r}\) is differentiable at \(t_0\) and
\begin{equation*} (f \circ \mathbf{r})'(t_0) = f_x(\mathbf{a}) r_1'(t_0) + f_y(\mathbf{a}) r_2'(t_0) = \nabla f(\mathbf{a}) \cdot \mathbf{r}'(t_0)\text{,} \end{equation*}
where \(\mathbf{a} = \mathbf{r}(t_0)\text{,}\) and \(r_1\) and \(r_2\) are the components of \(\mathbf{r}\text{.}\)
🔗
🔗
Why is this true?.
Let \(\mathbf{a}=\mathbf{r}(t_0)\text{.}\) For a small scalar change \(h\) in the parameter, the local linear approximation of the curve from Note 3.4.16 gives
\begin{equation*} \mathbf{r}(t_0+h)\approx\mathbf{a}+\mathbf{r}'(t_0)h. \end{equation*}
Thus the change in the input to \(f\) is approximately \(\mathbf{r}'(t_0)h\text{.}\)
🔗
As explained in the proof of Theorem 3.4.5, the local linear approximation of \(f\) near \(\mathbf{a}\) is
\begin{equation*} f(\mathbf{a}+\mathbf{k})\approx f(\mathbf{a})+\nabla f(\mathbf{a})\cdot\mathbf{k}. \end{equation*}
Substitute the actual input change \(\mathbf{k}=\mathbf{r}(t_0+h)-\mathbf{a}\text{,}\) then use the approximation from the curve:
\begin{align*} (f\circ\mathbf{r})(t_0+h)\amp\approx f(\mathbf{a})+\nabla f(\mathbf{a})\cdot\bigl(\mathbf{r}(t_0+h)-\mathbf{a}\bigr)\\ \amp\approx f(\mathbf{a})+\bigl(\nabla f(\mathbf{a})\cdot\mathbf{r}'(t_0)\bigr)h. \end{align*}
🔗
We have obtained the local affine approximation
\begin{equation*} L(t)=f(\mathbf{a})+\bigl(\nabla f(\mathbf{a})\cdot\mathbf{r}'(t_0)\bigr)(t-t_0). \end{equation*}
Its linear part multiplies the parameter change by \(\nabla f(\mathbf{a})\cdot\mathbf{r}'(t_0)\text{.}\) Reading off this coefficient gives
\begin{equation*} (f\circ\mathbf{r})'(t_0)=f_x(\mathbf{a})r_1'(t_0)+f_y(\mathbf{a})r_2'(t_0)=\nabla f(\mathbf{a})\cdot\mathbf{r}'(t_0). \end{equation*}
Differentiability ensures that the neglected errors are smaller than first order in \(h\text{,}\) so they do not change this coefficient.
