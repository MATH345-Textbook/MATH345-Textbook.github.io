Activity 3.4.3. Predicting a change in fitting error (U3-LO4).
For the prediction loss in Activity 3.1.9, Activity 3.3.36 gives
\begin{equation*} \mathcal L(\mathbf{w}_0)=1,\qquad \nabla\mathcal L(\mathbf{w}_0)=\begin{bmatrix}0\\-2\end{bmatrix},\qquad \mathbf{w}_0=(1,1). \end{equation*}
Suppose we increase both weights by \(1/10\text{,}\) so \(\mathbf{h}=(1/10,1/10)\text{.}\)
🔗
  1. Find the affine approximation \(\widehat{\mathcal L}(\mathbf{w})\) near \(\mathbf{w}_0\text{.}\) Use it to predict the change in loss and the new loss at \(\mathbf{w}_0+\mathbf{h}\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗
  2. Compute the new predictions \(X(\mathbf{w}_0+\mathbf{h})\text{,}\) residuals, and exact loss. Compare the exact loss with the affine prediction.
    🔗
    🔗
  3. For an arbitrary change \(\mathbf{h}=(h_1,h_2)\text{,}\) expand \(\mathcal L(\mathbf{w}_0+\mathbf{h})\text{.}\) Show that the approximation error is \(\|X\mathbf{h}\|^2\text{.}\) What happens to this error if the change is halved?
    🔗
    🔗
Solution.
  1. The approximation is
    \begin{equation*} \widehat{\mathcal L}(\mathbf{w}) =1+\begin{bmatrix}0\amp-2\end{bmatrix} \begin{bmatrix}w_1-1\\w_2-1\end{bmatrix} =1-2(w_2-1). \end{equation*}
    The predicted change is \(-2/10=-1/5\text{,}\) and the predicted new loss is \(4/5=0.8\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗
  2. At the new weights \((11/10,11/10)\text{,}\)
    \begin{align*} X(\mathbf{w}_0+\mathbf{h})\amp=\begin{bmatrix}11/10\\11/10\\11/5\end{bmatrix},\\ R(\mathbf{w}_0+\mathbf{h})\amp=\begin{bmatrix}1/10\\-9/10\\1/5\end{bmatrix}. \end{align*}
    The exact loss is \(1/100+81/100+1/25=43/50=0.86\text{.}\) The loss decreases, but the affine approximation underestimates the new loss by \(3/50=0.06\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗
  3. Expanding the three squared residuals gives
    \begin{align*} \mathcal L(\mathbf{w}_0+\mathbf{h})\amp=h_1^2+(-1+h_2)^2+(h_1+h_2)^2\\ \amp=1-2h_2+h_1^2+h_2^2+(h_1+h_2)^2. \end{align*}
    The first two terms give the affine approximation. The remaining terms are
    \begin{equation*} \left\|\begin{bmatrix}h_1\\h_2\\h_1+h_2\end{bmatrix}\right\|^2 =\|X\mathbf{h}\|^2. \end{equation*}
    Halving \(\mathbf{h}\) halves \(X\mathbf{h}\text{,}\) so its squared length is quartered. Unit 1’s matrix-vector product gives the exact prediction change; the loss change also contains these quadratic terms.
