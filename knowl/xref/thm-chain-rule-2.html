Theorem 3.5.11. Chain Rule for Two Independent Variables.
Suppose \(f: \R^2 \to \R\) is a scalar-valued function, and \(G: \R^2 \to \R^2\) is a vector-valued function. Then \(f \circ G: \R^2 \to \R\) is a scalar-valued function, and the two equations
\begin{equation*} (f \circ G)_x = (f_x \circ G) (G_1)_x + (f_y \circ G) (G_2)_x \end{equation*}
and
\begin{equation*} (f \circ G)_y = (f_x \circ G) (G_1)_y + (f_y \circ G) (G_2)_y \end{equation*}
hold.
🔗
🔗
Why is this true?.
Fix a base point \(\mathbf{a}=(x_0,y_0)\) and write \(\mathbf{b}=G(\mathbf{a})=(b_1,b_2)\text{.}\) For a small input change \(\mathbf{h}=(h_1,h_2)\text{,}\) use Note 3.4.18 to approximate each component of \(G\text{:}\)
\begin{equation*} G_i(\mathbf{a}+\mathbf{h})\approx b_i+(G_i)_x(\mathbf{a})h_1+(G_i)_y(\mathbf{a})h_2,\qquad i=1,2. \end{equation*}
🔗
The actual change in the input to \(f\) is \(\mathbf{k}=G(\mathbf{a}+\mathbf{h})-\mathbf{b}\text{.}\) Near \(\mathbf{b}\text{,}\) the local linear approximation of \(f\) gives
\begin{equation*} f(\mathbf{b}+\mathbf{k})\approx f(\mathbf{b})+f_x(\mathbf{b})k_1+f_y(\mathbf{b})k_2. \end{equation*}
Substitute the approximations for \(k_1\) and \(k_2\) from the two components of \(G\text{,}\) and collect the terms in \(h_1\) and \(h_2\text{:}\)
\begin{align*} (f\circ G)(\mathbf{a}+\mathbf{h})\amp\approx f(\mathbf{b})+\bigl[f_x(\mathbf{b})(G_1)_x(\mathbf{a})+f_y(\mathbf{b})(G_2)_x(\mathbf{a})\bigr]h_1\\ \amp\quad+\bigl[f_x(\mathbf{b})(G_1)_y(\mathbf{a})+f_y(\mathbf{b})(G_2)_y(\mathbf{a})\bigr]h_2. \end{align*}
🔗
This is an affine approximation: the constant term is \(f(\mathbf{b})=(f\circ G)(\mathbf{a})\text{,}\) and the remaining terms are linear in the input change. The coefficient of \(h_1\) gives the partial derivative with respect to the first input, and the coefficient of \(h_2\) gives the partial derivative with respect to the second input. Reading off these coefficients gives
\begin{align*} (f\circ G)_x(\mathbf{a})\amp=f_x(\mathbf{b})(G_1)_x(\mathbf{a})+f_y(\mathbf{b})(G_2)_x(\mathbf{a}),\\ (f\circ G)_y(\mathbf{a})\amp=f_x(\mathbf{b})(G_1)_y(\mathbf{a})+f_y(\mathbf{b})(G_2)_y(\mathbf{a}). \end{align*}
Since \(\mathbf{b}=G(\mathbf{a})\) and the base point is arbitrary, these are the two formulas in the theorem. Differentiability ensures that the neglected errors are smaller than first order in \(\mathbf{h}\text{,}\) so they do not change the linear part.
