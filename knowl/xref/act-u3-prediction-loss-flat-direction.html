Activity 3.6.28. Redundant features give a flat direction of the loss (U3-LO6, U2-LO6).

Recall Unit 2’s redundant-feature example in Activity 2.1.7. Add a third feature to our data by summing the original two feature columns:
\begin{equation*} \widetilde X=\begin{bmatrix}1\amp0\amp1\\0\amp1\amp1\\1\amp1\amp2\end{bmatrix}, \qquad \mathbf{y}=\begin{bmatrix}1\\2\\2\end{bmatrix}. \end{equation*}
There are now three weights \(\boldsymbol\theta=(\theta_1,\theta_2,\theta_3)\text{,}\) with loss
\begin{equation*} \widetilde{\mathcal L}(\boldsymbol\theta)=\|\widetilde X\boldsymbol\theta-\mathbf{y}\|^2. \end{equation*}
The predictions depend only on the two combinations \(w_1=\theta_1+\theta_3\) and \(w_2=\theta_2+\theta_3\text{,}\) so \(\widetilde{\mathcal L}(\boldsymbol\theta)=\mathcal L(\theta_1+\theta_3,\theta_2+\theta_3)\text{.}\)
🔗
  1. Verify that \(\mathbf{z}=(-1,-1,1)\) is in the null space of \(\widetilde X\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗
  2. Show that \(\widetilde{\mathcal L}(\boldsymbol\theta+t\mathbf{z})=\widetilde{\mathcal L}(\boldsymbol\theta)\) for every real \(t\text{.}\) Evaluate this constant loss along the line through \(\boldsymbol\theta_0=(1,1,0)\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗
  3. What is \(\frac{d}{dt}\widetilde{\mathcal L}(\boldsymbol\theta+t\mathbf{z})\text{?}\) If a minimizing weight vector is found, can it be the unique minimizing weight vector? Compare this exact invariance with the local Jacobian example in Activity 3.4.25.
    🔗
    🔗
Solution.
  1. Direct multiplication verifies the given direction:
    \begin{equation*} \widetilde X\mathbf{z} =\begin{bmatrix}-1+1\\-1+1\\-1-1+2\end{bmatrix} =\mathbf{0}. \end{equation*}
    Decreasing each original weight by \(t\) and increasing the redundant-feature weight by \(t\) leaves both effective weights unchanged.
    🔗
    🔗
  2. Unit 2’s linearity identity gives
    \begin{equation*} \widetilde X(\boldsymbol\theta+t\mathbf{z})-\mathbf{y} =\widetilde X\boldsymbol\theta-\mathbf{y} +t\widetilde X\mathbf{z} =\widetilde X\boldsymbol\theta-\mathbf{y}. \end{equation*}
    Taking squared lengths proves equality of the losses for every \(t\text{.}\) At \(\boldsymbol\theta_0\text{,}\) the predictions are \((1,1,2)\) and the loss is \(1\text{.}\) Every point \((1-t,1-t,t)\) gives these same predictions and the same loss.
    🔗
    🔗
  3. The loss along the line is constant, so its derivative is zero for every \(t\text{.}\) By the chain rule, \(\nabla\widetilde{\mathcal L}(\boldsymbol\theta)^T\mathbf{z}=0\text{.}\) If \(\boldsymbol\theta_*\) minimizes the loss, all distinct vectors \(\boldsymbol\theta_*+t\mathbf{z}\) minimize it as well. The data therefore cannot identify these three weights uniquely.
    🔗
    This flat direction is exact because the prediction map is linear and \(\widetilde X\mathbf{z}=\mathbf{0}\text{.}\) For a general nonlinear map, a vector in the null space of its Jacobian at one point need only give zero first-order change; higher-order terms may still change the output. Also, equal scalar losses alone need not mean equal predictions, as the two choices in Activity 3.1.9 showed.
