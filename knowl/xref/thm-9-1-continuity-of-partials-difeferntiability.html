Theorem 3.4.5. Continuity of First Partials Implies Differentiability.
Let \(f\) be a scalar-valued function, and suppose \(\mathbf{a}\) is an interior point (recall Definition 3.1.28) of the domains of \(f\) and its partial derivatives. Moreover, suppose that all of the partial derivatives \(f_{x_1}, \dots, f_{x_n}\) are continuous at \(\mathbf{a}\text{.}\) Then \(f\) is differentiable at \(\mathbf{a}\text{.}\)
🔗
🔗
Why is this true?.
Here is the intuition in two variables. To move from \((a,b)\) to a nearby point \((a+h,b+k)\text{,}\) change one coordinate at a time:
\begin{equation*} (a,b) \longrightarrow (a+h,b) \longrightarrow (a+h,b+k). \end{equation*}
On each step, only one variable changes, so we can use a one-variable linear approximation. Continuity of the partial derivatives means that the slopes along these small steps stay close to their values at \((a,b)\text{.}\) Thus
\begin{align*} f(a+h,b)-f(a,b) \amp\approx f_x(a,b)h,\\ f(a+h,b+k)-f(a+h,b) \amp\approx f_y(a,b)k. \end{align*}
🔗
Adding the two changes cancels the value at the intermediate point and gives
\begin{equation*} f(a+h,b+k)-f(a,b) \approx f_x(a,b)h+f_y(a,b)k. \end{equation*}
Adding \(f(a,b)\) to both sides gives an approximation for the value at the new point:
\begin{equation*} f(a+h,b+k) \approx f(a,b)+f_x(a,b)h+f_y(a,b)k. \end{equation*}
This explains both the sum and its coefficients: each partial derivative measures the change per unit step in one coordinate. Continuity is what lets us use the slopes at the base point throughout the nearby steps; each small error in a slope is multiplied by the corresponding small coordinate change.
🔗
In \(n\) variables, the same idea uses \(n\) coordinate steps. Adding their contributions gives the linear approximation
\begin{align*} L(\mathbf{a}+\mathbf{h}) \amp= f(\mathbf{a})+\sum_{i=1}^n f_{x_i}(\mathbf{a})h_i\\ \amp= f(\mathbf{a})+\nabla f(\mathbf{a})\cdot\mathbf{h}. \end{align*}
